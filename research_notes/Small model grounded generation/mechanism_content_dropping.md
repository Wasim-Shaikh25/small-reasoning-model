# Mechanistic explanation: why small FLAN-T5-family seq2seq models drop/omit input content rather than inventing it

## Key Question 1: What does the T5/FLAN-T5 training objective (span-corruption pretraining + short-answer instruction-tuning) imply about the model's learned prior over output length / how much of the input to reproduce?

### Takeaway
T5's pretraining objective is explicitly engineered to produce **short decoder targets** (a design choice made for compute efficiency, not length fidelity), and FLAN instruction-tuning layers on a task mixture that is itself skewed toward short responses — both stages push the model's learned prior toward truncated, under-generated output rather than toward faithfully reproducing/expanding all input content.

### Cited Findings
- T5's span-corruption objective corrupts contiguous spans (not individual tokens, unlike BERT) and replaces each with a single sentinel token; the decoder only has to regenerate the dropped spans plus their sentinels, not the full input — making decoder targets much shorter than the input by construction. — [T5 paper discussion / HF forum quoting Raffel et al. 2020](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/preprocessing-for-t5-denoising/6266)
- Raffel et al. explicitly chose "a mean span length of 3 and corrupt 15% of the original sequence," and state this objective was selected in part because it is "slightly more computationally efficient due to shorter target sequence lengths" (quoting Table 7 ablations). — [Raffel et al. 2020, "Exploring the Limits of Transfer Learning" (arXiv 1910.10683), as quoted on HF forum](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/preprocessing-for-t5-denoising/6266)
- A concrete T5 pretraining configuration shows a 568-token (expanded) input paired with only a 114-token target — illustrating how large the input:target length asymmetry is baked into pretraining. — [Cerebras T5 docs](https://docs.cerebras.net/en/latest/wsc/port/_autosummary/cerebras.modelzoo.data.nlp.t5.t5_utils.html)
- The UT5 paper (non-autoregressive T5 pretraining) notes, as a stated hypothesis, that "a single span corruption target is short, hence less meaningful to unroll" — i.e., researchers studying T5's own objective treat short targets as an inherent, limiting property of span corruption. — [UT5: Pretraining Non-autoregressive T5 with Unrolled Denoising (arXiv 2311.08552)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.08552)
- FLAN/instruction-tuning data is documented elsewhere as skewed toward short sequences: a NeurIPS 2023 supplementary analysis reports that instruction-tuning datasets (including the FLAN CoT subset introduced by Longpre et al.'s Flan Collection) have a short median instruction/response length. — [NeurIPS 2023 supplemental material](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/4e85362c02172c0c6567ce593122d31c-Supplemental-Conference.pdf)
- Chung et al.'s FLAN-T5/Flan-PaLM paper (the source of the flan-t5-small checkpoint) instruction-tunes on a large mixture (1.8K tasks for the flagship model) spanning classification, short-form QA, and reasoning — a task mixture historically dominated by short-answer formats (NLU benchmarks, classification, extractive QA) rather than long free-form generation. — [Chung et al., "Scaling Instruction-Finetuned Language Models" (arXiv 2210.11416 / JMLR 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11416v3)
- An empirical probe of FLAN-T5 on a story-ending generation task found FLAN-T5-small defaults to very short outputs (~9.8 words unconstrained) and is largely **insensitive to explicit length instructions** — producing ~10.2–10.3 words whether told to write 10 or 15 words — in contrast to decoder-only LLMs (Llama2-7B/Llama3-8B) which visibly shortened output when instructed. This indicates a strong, instruction-resistant prior toward brevity in FLAN-T5-small specifically. — [Evaluation of Instruction-Following Ability for LLMs on Story-Ending Generation (arXiv 2406.16356)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.16356)

### Inferences
- Because the pretraining objective rewards generating only the "missing/corrupted" portion of a sequence, the model never learns, at the pretraining stage, a strong objective pressure to **reproduce long stretches of given content verbatim**; its base skill is "predict a short gap-filler," not "faithfully carry forward everything you were shown." Instruction-tuning on mostly short-answer tasks reinforces rather than corrects this, since few FLAN tasks require long, multi-fact, content-preserving generation.
- For a bounded compression/rewrite task (combine N sentences into one paragraph), this prior manifests as the model treating the task like one of its short-answer FLAN tasks: it tends to produce a plausible-length "answer" calibrated to its learned output-length distribution rather than a length proportional to total input content, and the easiest way to hit a short target is to select/paraphrase a subset of facts and silently drop the rest.
- Parameter count interacts with this: smaller models have less capacity to learn an exception to the dominant short-output prior for the rarer long-generation tasks in the mixture, so this truncation bias should be most visible at the "small" scale (see Key Question 6).

### Gaps
- No source directly measures "input:output length ratio" or fact-retention rate for FLAN-T5-small on compression/rewrite tasks specifically (e.g., controlled experiments varying the number of input sentences and measuring how many are retained). This specific mechanistic coupling (short-target pretraining prior → fact-dropping on multi-fact fusion) is my inference from the cited findings above, not something any single source demonstrates end-to-end.
- No source gives a quantitative breakdown of the FLAN task mixture by output-length distribution (e.g., % of tasks requiring >50-token outputs) to confirm "mostly short-answer" as more than a qualitative characterization.

## Key Question 2: What role does exposure bias (teacher forcing vs. autoregressive inference) play in content-dropping for seq2seq models specifically, vs. decoder-only LLMs?

### Takeaway
Exposure bias is a well-documented train/inference mismatch (teacher forcing on gold prefixes vs. conditioning on the model's own, possibly erroneous, prefix at inference) that is architecture-agnostic in its basic mechanism, but no source directly quantifies its specific contribution to *content omission* (as opposed to general quality degradation) in seq2seq summarization — this causal link is inferred, not measured, in the literature found.

### Cited Findings
- Exposure bias arises because "the gold summary's tokens being unavailable at inference, so they have to be replaced by the generated tokens," meaning training never exposes the model to its own mistakes, so it is optimized under a limited part of the state space that can degrade test-time performance. — [Alleviating Exposure Bias via Contrastive Learning for Abstractive Text Summarization (arXiv 2108.11846)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.11846)
- Sun and Li report models trained this way are "very likely to generate low-quality summaries as a result," and the problem is tied to maximum-likelihood training itself, which "often yields suboptimal results on metrics such as ROUGE." — [arXiv 2108.11846](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.11846)
- Countervailing evidence: He et al. identify a "self-recovery" ability of MLE-trained language models that casts doubt on how large a problem exposure bias really is in practice, and propose metrics to quantify (rather than assume) its impact. — [cited via search summary; original: He et al. on self-recovery from exposure-bias-induced errors](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.10617v5)
- On architecture-specific differences: a direct comparison paper on decoder-only vs. encoder-decoder models for seq2seq tasks reports a distinct, related failure in decoder-only setups — "as the generation step number grows, less and less attention is focused on the source sequence" ("attention degeneration"), where the ever-growing generated prefix increasingly competes with the (fixed) source for attention budget. — [Decoder-Only or Encoder-Decoder? Interpreting Language Model as a Regularized Encoder-Decoder (arXiv 2304.04052)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.04052)
- Scheduled sampling (a standard exposure-bias mitigation for RNN seq2seq) is harder to apply to Transformer-based models (both encoder-decoder and decoder-only) because "the generation of a new word attends to the full sentence generated so far, not only to the last word," unlike RNNs — complicating simple exposure-bias fixes for modern seq2seq architectures including T5. — [Scheduled Sampling for Transformers (Mihaylova & Martins 2019), via search summary](https://deepai.org/publication/scheduled-sampling-for-transformers)

### Inferences
- In an encoder-decoder model like T5, the source document is encoded once and is available to the decoder via cross-attention at every decoding step, regardless of exposure-bias-induced drift in the decoder's own generated prefix. This is architecturally different from decoder-only models, where the source competes with the growing generated prefix inside one single attention mechanism (the "attention degeneration" effect above) — so encoder-decoder models should, in principle, be *less* susceptible to losing track of the source as generation proceeds purely due to exposure bias, compared to decoder-only models.
- Given that structural protection, exposure bias alone is a weaker candidate explanation for seq2seq-specific content-dropping than the length/compression prior (Key Question 1) or cross-attention coverage limits (Key Question 3). Its likely contribution is indirect: once a small decoder makes an early token choice that implicitly "closes off" covering a remaining fact (e.g., committing to a sentence-final clause structure), teacher-forcing-trained models have had no practice recovering from that kind of self-induced commitment, so they are more likely to just end the sentence/paragraph — manifesting as omission rather than a correction.

### Gaps
- No source was found that isolates exposure bias from other causes (length prior, cross-attention capacity, beam search) in a controlled way for *content omission specifically*, in any seq2seq model, let alone FLAN-T5-small. The "self-recovery" counter-finding (He et al.) suggests the overall seq2seq literature is unsettled on how much exposure bias matters in practice, which weakens confidence in exposure bias as a primary mechanism here.
- No decoder-only vs. encoder-decoder controlled comparison measuring *omission rates specifically* (as opposed to general fluency/BLEU) was found.

## Key Question 3: How does cross-attention over a multi-sentence input work in T5-style encoder-decoder architectures, and is there documented research on cross-attention failing to attend to all input facts equally?

### Takeaway
Yes — there is direct empirical evidence that encoder-decoder cross-attention in transformer summarizers is **sparse and concentrated on a small subset of input sentences** rather than spread evenly across all source content, which is a plausible direct mechanism for why multi-fact inputs lose facts that fall outside the attention-dominant region.

### Cited Findings
- Manakul and Gales (EMNLP 2021) directly studied encoder-decoder cross-attention in transformer summarizers across CNN/DailyMail, XSum, Spotify Podcast, and arXiv, finding attention is "concentrated on a small part of the source" — a "sparse sentence structure" that they could exploit by restricting attention to a chosen subset of input sentences while preserving performance. — [Sparsity and Sentence Structure in Encoder-Decoder Attention of Summarization Systems, EMNLP 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.739)
- A separate analysis (Siragusa and Robaldo, 2022) argues the issue is structural: a "plain encoder-decoder model treats words and sentences equally, without separating the most relevant ones from the rest," proposing an added sentence-level (PageRank-scored) attention layer specifically to address uneven/unprincipled coverage across sentences. — [Applied Sciences 2022, via search summary](https://doaj.org/article/33ba2ffba3e34d2797000bf0a6858143)
- Earlier work (Feng et al., CIKM 2018) found that standard attention-based encoder-decoder summarizers "usually build document representations from the sentences that come before a given sentence" and so "miss relationships with the sentences that follow it" — a directional/positional bias in how multi-sentence input is integrated.
- The pointer-generator "coverage mechanism" literature (See, Liu, Manning 2017) was built specifically because seq2seq attention, left unconstrained, tends to re-attend to the same already-used source content across decoding steps rather than moving on to new content, and this problem is "especially pronounced when generating multi-sentence text" — i.e., multi-sentence/multi-fact generation specifically stresses the attention mechanism's ability to track what has and hasn't been used from the source. — [Get To The Point: Summarization with Pointer-Generator Networks, ACL 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.04368)
- Caveat found in the search: the sparsity/concentration findings describe attention being dominated by a *subset* of sentences, not literally "only one sentence," and whether attention weights faithfully reflect what information the model actually uses downstream is a separate, debated question not resolved by these sources.

### Inferences
- For a bounded fusion task (combine 3–5 short factual sentences into one paragraph), if cross-attention in a small model concentrates on 1–2 of the input sentences (consistent with the Manakul & Gales finding), the facts in the under-attended sentences are architecturally starved of the attention-mediated signal needed to surface in the output — a direct mechanistic path to omission that does not require any model "error" in the sense of incorrect token prediction; it's a capacity/allocation problem in the attention distribution itself.
- Smaller models (fewer heads, smaller hidden dimension in cross-attention) have less representational capacity to maintain separate "coverage state" for each input fact simultaneously, which would plausibly make this concentration effect worse at small scale — consistent with coverage mechanisms (explicit bookkeeping of what's been attended to) being introduced specifically to compensate for this architectural limitation.

### Gaps
- No source specifically studies cross-attention concentration in T5 or FLAN-T5 (the studies found used custom/non-T5 transformer summarizers on CNN/DM, XSum, Spotify Podcast, arXiv). Generalization to T5's relative-position-biased attention and to the ~77M-parameter small scale is inferred, not confirmed.
- No source measures attention concentration as a function of model size, so the claim that smaller models show *more* concentrated/uneven attention is my inference, not a cited finding.

## Key Question 4: Is there research specifically on multi-document or multi-fact fusion/aggregation in small seq2seq summarization models showing content-coverage failures (missing facts)?

### Takeaway
Yes — multi-document summarization (MDS) research shows that models (and even the datasets/tasks themselves) systematically under-use all-but-one-or-two source documents, and sentence-fusion analyses show that end-to-end-trained abstractive models rarely perform genuine multi-sentence fusion at all, instead defaulting to reproducing a dominant single source — both point to a general "single-source-dominance" failure pattern in fact aggregation, though the coverage side (vs. faithfulness side) is less directly quantified than the fusion-rate side.

### Cited Findings
- Wolhandler et al. (EMNLP 2022) measured how many source documents are actually needed to reconstruct a reference summary in MultiNews (4 documents per topic): a single document alone covered ~70% of summary propositions, and two documents covered ~95% — meaning "the remaining source documents thus hardly provide any substantial information to the summary." This shows that even the *reference* summaries, and models trained to match them, don't require genuine multi-source fusion in most cases — a dataset-level explanation for why models learn to privilege one dominant source and drop facts unique to the others. — [How "Multi" is Multi-Document Summarization?, EMNLP 2022 (arXiv 2210.12688)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.12688)
- A 2025 thesis on multi-source summarization reports that such systems "often prioritise frequently repeated information while overlooking contextually important but less common facts" — i.e., aggregation models have a learned bias toward redundant/majority content over unique per-source facts, which directly predicts omission of facts that only appear once in the input (as in a bounded fact-combination task where each sentence contributes a unique fact). — [Multi-Source Document Summarisation for Coverage, thesis, 2025](https://trepo.tuni.fi/handle/10024/231952)
- "Do Multi-Document Summarization Models Synthesize?" (arXiv 2301.13844) finds models are "sensitive to missing inputs" and argues prior "sentence fusion" work has implicitly assumed the goal is to extract information *common* to similar input sentences — a framing that is poorly suited to combining *distinct, non-overlapping* facts (exactly the structure of a bounded fact-combination task), predicting that unique/non-redundant facts are the ones most likely to be dropped. — [Do Multi-Document Summarization Models Synthesize?, 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.13844)
- Lebanoff et al. (2019) found genuine sentence fusion is rare in trained abstractive summarizers: only ~6% of Pointer-Generator's output sentences were produced via fusion of multiple source sentences, versus 32% in human-written abstracts — because end-to-end training rewards ROUGE/word-overlap with a reference, not correct multi-sentence fusion per se, leaving the fusion *skill* underdeveloped relative to single-source extraction/paraphrase. — [Analyzing Sentence Fusion in Abstractive Summarization, EMNLP 2019 Workshop (D19-5413)](https://research.adobe.com/publication/analyzing-sentence-fusion-in-abstractive-summarization/)
- The same fusion-error analysis found that when models *do* attempt fusion, a common failure is misattribution/addition (e.g., incorrectly combining two people's situations into one sentence) — i.e., the fusion errors documented in this literature skew toward hallucination-style errors, not omission, which is a useful contrast rather than direct support for the omission question. — [same source, D19-5413](https://research.adobe.com/publication/analyzing-sentence-fusion-in-abstractive-summarization/)

### Inferences
- The MDS literature's core lesson — that models (and the data they're trained on) default to privileging one dominant source/fact and treat the rest as optional — generalizes directly to the user's bounded "combine several short factual sentences" task: a FLAN-T5-small facing N short, roughly equally-weighted factual sentences has no training signal that teaches it all N must appear; its closest learned analogy (single-document summarization / short-answer QA) rewards picking a subset.
- This is a distinct mechanism from cross-attention sparsity (Key Question 3): even with perfect attention, a model trained predominantly on tasks where partial/majority coverage was "good enough" for the loss has no incentive gradient pushing it toward exhaustive coverage. The two mechanisms (attention capacity and training incentive) likely compound in a 77M-parameter model.

### Gaps
- No source directly studies *small/77M-scale* seq2seq models on MDS or fact-fusion coverage; the cited MDS studies do not report model size as a variable, so the small-model-specific amplification of this effect is inferred, not demonstrated.
- No source gives a direct, controlled "coverage rate vs. number of input facts" curve for any seq2seq model doing a bounded combination task resembling the user's described use case (combining several short factual sentences into one paragraph).

## Key Question 5: Does beam search have a documented tendency toward shorter, "safer" outputs that drop content, and is this exacerbated in small models?

### Takeaway
Yes, strongly documented: beam search (and likelihood-maximizing decoding generally) has a well-established, measured bias toward shorter outputs and even degenerate empty outputs, rooted in the underlying model's own scoring preferences rather than being purely a search artifact — this is one of the most directly evidenced mechanisms for content-dropping found in this research, though the small-model-scale amplification is argued rather than directly measured for FLAN-T5.

### Cited Findings
- Stahlberg and Byrne (EMNLP 2019) used exact search on a Transformer NMT model and found beam search (even with beam size 100) usually fails to find the model's actual global-best-scoring output; strikingly, "for more than 50% of the sentences, the model in fact assigns its global best score to the empty translation" — demonstrating the bias toward brevity/emptiness is a property of what the *model itself* considers optimal, not merely a beam-search search error. — [On NMT Search Errors and Model Errors: Cat Got Your Tongue?, EMNLP 2019 (D19-1331)](https://preview.aclanthology.org/moar-dois/D19-1331.pdf)
- They confirmed the root cause is a built-in model preference for shorter outputs by constraining search to a minimum length, which fixed the problem — "at the root of the problem of empty translations lies an inherent bias towards shorter translations." With exact search unconstrained, BLEU collapsed to 2.1 (vs. 30.3 for beam-10) with a length ratio of only 0.06 and ~52% empty outputs. — [same source](https://preview.aclanthology.org/moar-dois/D19-1331.pdf)
- Holtzman et al. (ICLR 2020) independently show that maximization-based decoding (beam search, greedy, top-k with high k) produces degenerate, repetitive, or overly generic/bland text, and specifically that with larger beam widths (64+), GPT-2 Large/XL models "prefer to stop generating immediately after the given context" — a direct documented case of larger search budgets making the shortness/stopping problem *worse*, not better. — [The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration, ICLR 2020 (arXiv 1904.09751)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.09751)
- A related WMT 2018 workshop paper ties beam degradation and overly short output to a common root cause, "label bias," and reports that correcting the brevity problem "almost eliminates the beam problem" — reinforcing that length-bias and beam-search misbehavior are mechanistically linked, not independent issues. — [Correcting Length Bias in Neural Machine Translation, WMT 2018, via search summary](https://cs.paperswithcode.com/paper/correcting-length-bias-in-neural-machine)
- Provilkov and Malinin (2021) trace part of the root cause to training data itself: "NMT datasets are strongly biased towards short sentences," proposing training-data resampling (concatenating multiple sentences) as a fix — connecting the decoding-time brevity bias back to a training-data-level cause, analogous to the short-target bias discussed in Key Question 1 for T5's pretraining objective.

### Inferences
- These findings, while from NMT, describe a general property of likelihood-trained seq2seq models under maximization-based decoding (beam search / greedy): the model's own probability landscape favors shorter sequences, and beam search (the default decoding strategy for most HuggingFace seq2seq pipelines, including typical FLAN-T5 usage) searches for high-probability — i.e., preferentially shorter — sequences. Applied to a bounded fusion task, this predicts exactly the observed symptom: the model stops generating (ending the paragraph) once it has covered enough facts to reach a "good enough" (high per-token-average-probability) sequence under its length-normalized beam score, dropping remaining facts rather than extending the output to include them.
- Small models are plausibly more susceptible because (a) they have less capacity to learn precise length-calibration for each specific input (so the brevity bias is less "corrected" by context-specific learned signal), and (b) the FLAN-T5-small training mixture (per Key Question 1) reinforces a short-output prior that compounds with — rather than counteracts — beam search's independent brevity bias. No source directly tests this interaction for FLAN-T5 at varying scale, so this compounding claim is inferential.

### Gaps
- No source runs the exact-search / minimum-length-constraint experiment (à la Stahlberg & Byrne) on T5/FLAN-T5 or on any encoder-decoder *summarization* model (as opposed to NMT) — generalization of the "model itself prefers emptiness" finding from translation to compression/fusion tasks is plausible but unconfirmed.
- No source compares beam search's brevity bias magnitude across FLAN-T5 scales (small vs. base vs. large vs. xl).

## Key Question 6: Is this failure mode specific to small model scale, or more about T5's training objective/architecture than parameter count?

### Takeaway
No source directly benchmarks omission/coverage rates across the flan-t5-small/base/large/xl scale ladder, but the available indirect evidence (short-target pretraining objective shared across all T5 scales; dataset-level single-source dominance in MDS; general LLM-scale-faithfulness literature showing diminishing-but-nonzero returns from scale) suggests the failure mode is **rooted in the shared training objective and decoding setup (present at every T5 scale)**, while raw parameter count determines how well a given model can partially compensate for it — implying the bias should attenuate, not disappear, with scale.

### Cited Findings
- The span-corruption pretraining objective and its short-target design (Key Question 1) is identical across all T5/FLAN-T5 checkpoint sizes (small/base/large/xl/xxl) — it is a property of the T5 pretraining *recipe*, not of the small checkpoint specifically. — [Raffel et al. 2020, as discussed above](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/preprocessing-for-t5-denoising/6266)
- Chung et al. report that scaling FLAN-T5 substantially improves downstream task performance: "Flan-T5 11B outperforms T5 11B by double-digit improvements and even outperforms PaLM 62B on some challenging BIG-Bench tasks," showing instruction-tuned seq2seq models do get real capability gains from scale, which plausibly includes better-calibrated output length/content-coverage behavior, though this is not isolated as a specific claim. — [Chung et al. 2022/2024, "Scaling Instruction-Finetuned Language Models"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11416v3)
- In adjacent large-model faithfulness literature, a multi-document summarization scaling study found that "lexical overlap and factual consistency follow distinct scaling dynamics" — faithfulness improves rapidly early in data/model scaling and then shows diminishing returns, while ROUGE-style overlap keeps climbing more smoothly — suggesting a floor/ceiling effect where scale helps but does not fully resolve content-fidelity issues, and ROUGE-based evaluation (which rewards overlap, not completeness) can mask remaining coverage problems even in larger models. — [cited via search summary of a multi-document summarization scaling preprint]
- TrueBrief (a 2025/2026-era framework for improving faithfulness specifically in small LLMs/SLMs via preference optimization) frames faithfulness in small models as a problem specifically worth solving with post-hoc intervention (preference optimization on synthetic hallucination-injected data) rather than something scale alone resolves, implicitly supporting that the issue is tractable but not simply a function of scale that will vanish as model size grows within the same family/training recipe. — [TrueBrief: Faithful Summarization through Small Language Models (arXiv, 2601.04212)](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.04212v1)
- The FLAN-T5-small length-insensitivity finding (Key Question 1) — that the small model can barely be steered off its default short-output length even by explicit instruction — is itself evidence that at least *some* part of this failure mode (rigidity of output length / resistance to following explicit "be longer/include everything" instructions) is scale-sensitive, since this kind of instruction-following flexibility is a capability known to generally improve with model scale in the instruction-tuning literature broadly (though the cited story-generation study did not itself test base/large/xl for comparison). — [arXiv 2406.16356](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.16356)

### Inferences
- The most defensible position given the evidence: the *root cause* (short-target span-corruption pretraining objective + short-answer-dominated instruction mixture + beam search's brevity bias) is architecture/training-recipe-level and present at every FLAN-T5 scale, so it is not "fixed" by scale in the sense of being absent at base/large/xl. However, parameter count affects the model's capacity to (a) maintain separate attention/coverage state per input fact, (b) learn more precise, context-conditional length calibration that overrides the generic brevity prior when the input clearly contains multiple facts, and (c) follow explicit instructions to be more thorough — all of which should make larger FLAN-T5 checkpoints omit content *less often*, even though the underlying bias-generating mechanisms are shared with flan-t5-small.
- This implies the user's observed failure mode in flan-t5-small is best understood as "the shared T5-family bias toward brevity/partial-coverage, manifesting maximally because the small model has the least spare capacity to counteract it" — not as a bug unique to the small checkpoint's training run.

### Gaps
- No controlled study comparing omission/fact-coverage rates specifically across flan-t5-small vs. base vs. large vs. xl on a held-out compression/fusion benchmark was found. This is the single biggest evidentiary gap for this key question, and the conclusion above is an inference from indirect, partially-analogous evidence (general instruction-tuning scaling results, MDS faithfulness-scaling dynamics in other model families, and one small-model length-insensitivity data point), not a directly cited finding.
- No source isolates how much of any observed scale-related improvement (if it exists) would come from (a) better coverage/less omission specifically, vs. (b) general fluency/grammaticality gains, vs. (c) better instruction-following that happens to include "don't drop facts" style instructions.
