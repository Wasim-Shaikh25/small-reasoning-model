# Copy Mechanisms and Constrained Decoding for Faithful Small-Model Rewriting

## How do copy mechanisms / pointer-generator networks work, and would retrofitting/fine-tuning a small T5-style model with an explicit copy mechanism plausibly fix content-dropping? Is there existing work combining T5/FLAN-T5 with copy mechanisms?

### Takeaway
Pointer-generator and copy mechanisms (CopyNet, See et al.'s Pointer-Generator Network, and the newer PROM) add an explicit "lift this span from the source" path alongside free generation, which directly targets content-dropping/hallucination; this is architecturally compatible with T5-style encoder-decoders, and at least one concrete retrofit onto a pretrained transformer encoder-decoder (PROM, built on PEGASUS) exists and reports faithfulness gains — but no source found specifically retrofits a copy mechanism onto T5/FLAN-T5, so that exact combination would need custom implementation.

### Cited Findings
- CopyNet (Gu et al., ACL 2016, arXiv:1603.06393): an encoder-decoder model whose decoder combines ordinary word generation with a copy operation that selects sub-sequences from the input and places them in the output; trained end-to-end despite the seemingly "hard" copy operation; reported to outperform regular RNN seq2seq "with remarkable margins" on text summarization, and is especially useful for out-of-vocabulary / entity-name tokens — [CopyNet (deepai mirror)](https://deepai.org/publication/incorporating-copying-mechanism-in-sequence-to-sequence-learning); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06393v3)
- See, Liu & Manning, "Get To The Point: Summarization with Pointer-Generator Networks" (ACL 2017, arXiv:1704.04368): targets the fact that standard seq2seq summarizers reproduce factual details inaccurately and repeat themselves; the pointer-generator is a hybrid — a pointing component copies words directly from the input while a separate generator produces novel words, with a learned mixing weight deciding the route at each decoding step; a coverage mechanism additionally tracks what has already been attended to, discouraging repetition; the model beat the prior abstractive state-of-the-art by ≥2 ROUGE points on CNN/DailyMail — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1704.04368); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/P17-1099/)
- A later analysis found that forcing See et al.'s pointer-generator model to generate instead of copy exposed only simple paraphrasing ability alongside factual inaccuracies and hallucinations — i.e., most of its faithfulness comes specifically from the copy path, not the generator — [search summary citing "To Point or Not to Point"](https://www.paperswithcode.com/paper/to-point-or-not-to-point-understanding-how)
- A 2019 EMNLP paper found the pointer-generator's copy mechanism creates "a strong bias towards extractive generations," and proposed letting the model edit copied tokens via a learned relation embedding rather than hard-copying — [Improving Latent Alignment in Text Summarization by Generalizing the Pointer Generator (EMNLP 2019, D19-1390)](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/D19-1390)
- PROM (Ma et al., arXiv:2305.06647, published LREC-COLING 2024): a phrase-level copying mechanism retrofitted onto a pretrained transformer encoder-decoder (built on PEGASUS). It adds an indicator layer marking which source n-grams can be copied and trains with an auxiliary copy-prediction loss. Reported results: significant improvements after fine-tuning on summarization benchmarks, "more reasonable copying," and a contribution to faithfulness; in zero-shot settings PROM is applied during self-supervised pretraining on raw corpora and sets new baselines across a wide range of summarization datasets — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.06647); [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2305.06647)
- A 2026 paper (BLooP, arXiv:2603.11415) characterizes the lineage explicitly: "since the emergence of the pointer-generator mechanism, researchers have explored advanced techniques to enhance summary quality using encoder-decoder transformer architectures such as BART," and separately notes PEGASUS's decoder already does implicit copying via attention-weighted averaging of encoder states. The same paper argues PROM's need for specialized gap-sentence-generation pretraining and extra parameters "makes it less suitable for zero-shot applications with existing LLMs" — [BLooP](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.11415)
- A 2021 study (arXiv:2106.10084, on subjective bias in abstractive summarization) trained a Pointer-Generator Network, a Transformer, and a 60M-parameter T5 side by side as baselines. It describes PGNet as "a strong baseline that utilizes copy mechanism to improve the readability of summary but also brings the copy-too-much problem," but does not add a copy mechanism to T5 itself — it is a baseline comparison, not a hybrid — [Subjective Bias in Abstractive Summarization](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.10084)
- No source found describes an explicit pointer/copy-mechanism retrofit specifically onto T5 or FLAN-T5 (as opposed to PEGASUS/BART-family models or fresh RNN/Transformer baselines). This is a genuine gap, not a negative result — it appears to simply be unexplored or unpublished territory for the T5 architecture specifically.

### Inferences
- Because T5 and PEGASUS share the standard transformer encoder-decoder architecture (encoder self-attention, cross-attention from decoder to encoder states, decoder self-attention), the PROM-style phrase-copy indicator + auxiliary loss approach should be architecturally transferable to T5/FLAN-T5 with a custom implementation — nothing in the described mechanism is PEGASUS-specific beyond the choice of pretraining objective.
- The finding that a pointer-generator's faithfulness comes specifically from its copy path (not its generator) suggests that retrofitting copy capability onto a small T5 is a *plausible* fix for content-dropping specifically for spans that should be copied verbatim (numbers, names, dates, citation markers) — but it requires model-architecture changes and retraining/fine-tuning, not just prompting or decoding-time tricks.
- The extractive-bias / "copy-too-much" problem reported for both PGNet and PROM-less copy models implies that a naive copy mechanism retrofit risks trading hallucination for excessive extractiveness (reduced fluency/paraphrase quality) — a tuning problem (mixing weight / coverage, as in See et al.) rather than a fundamental blocker.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence (positive or negative) on fine-tuning T5 or FLAN-T5 specifically with an added copy/pointer mechanism; this would need to be implemented and evaluated from scratch, or a targeted literature search beyond this scope (e.g., searching GitHub for "T5 pointer generator" implementations) would be needed.
- No quantitative faithfulness numbers (e.g., number/entity preservation rate) were found for PROM or CopyNet that would let you estimate expected gains at ~100M–1B parameter scale.

## What is lexically-constrained decoding / constrained beam search, and how does it work mechanically? What is NeuroLogic Decoding and NeuroLogic A*esque decoding, and how do they enforce required tokens/phrases appear in the output?

### Takeaway
Lexically-constrained decoding modifies beam search itself (not the model) to guarantee required words/phrases appear in the output, via three main mechanical families — Grid Beam Search (Hokamp & Liu), Dynamic Beam Allocation (Post & Vilar), and NeuroLogic Decoding/A*esque (Lu et al.) — all of which are model-agnostic, work with any token-by-token generator (including T5-style encoder-decoders), and differ mainly in computational cost and expressiveness of the constraint language.

### Cited Findings
- Grid Beam Search (Hokamp & Liu, ACL 2017, arXiv:1704.07138): modifies beam search so pre-specified lexical constraints end up in the output; works with any model that generates token-by-token and requires no modification of model parameters or training data; tested on interactive-predictive translation and NMT domain adaptation, with large quality improvements in interactive scenarios; decoding complexity is linear in the number of constraints, with effective beam size k × (C+1) — [ACL Anthology P17-1141](https://aclanthology.org/P17-1141/); [GitHub reference implementation](https://github.com/chrishokamp/constrained_decoding)
- Dynamic Beam Allocation (Post & Vilar, NAACL 2018, arXiv:1804.06609): addresses the cost problem — prior approaches were linear (Grid Beam Search) or exponential (Anderson et al. 2017's Constrained Beam Search) in the number of constraints. Its method groups hypotheses into "banks" by how many constraints they've satisfied and dynamically divides the beam across banks (analogous to stack grouping in phrase-based MT decoding), achieving O(1) complexity in the number of constraints. Implemented in Sockeye — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1804.06609); [ACL Anthology N18-1119](https://aclanthology.org/N18-1119/)
- NeuroLogic Decoding (Lu et al., NAACL 2021, arXiv:2010.12884): targets the finding that large pretrained LMs fine-tuned on task data do not reliably satisfy lexical constraints even with heavy supervision. Constraints are expressed in Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF): each literal requires a phrase's inclusion or exclusion, literals combine into disjunctive clauses, and all clauses must be satisfiable for a valid output. The search is beam-based: rather than keeping the plain top-k candidates, it prunes candidates whose clauses are violated, seeking fluent sequences where all clauses hold. Asymptotic runtime equals conventional beam search. On four benchmarks it beat prior approaches (including ones handling only a constraint subset), and unsupervised models using NeuroLogic Decoding often beat much larger supervised models using conventional decoding — [DeepAI mirror](https://deepai.org/publication/neurologic-decoding-un-supervised-neural-text-generation-with-predicate-logic-constraints); [official code](https://github.com/dh1105/neurologic_decoding)
- NeuroLogic A*esque Decoding (Lu, Welleck et al., NAACL 2022, arXiv:2112.08726): extends NeuroLogic Decoding by borrowing A* search's idea of incorporating a heuristic estimate of future cost, arguing constrained generation needs lookahead to plan feasible future paths. At each step, candidates are scored by combining the accumulated history score with an estimated future score (the log-likelihood of a short lookahead continuation), weighted by a trade-off parameter λ — analogous to weighted A* (Pohl, 1970). This lookahead heuristic is a drop-in replacement for plain beam search / top-k sampling within the same CNF-constraint framework. Reported to outperform competitive baselines on five generation tasks and set new state-of-the-art on table-to-text generation, constrained machine translation, and keyword-constrained generation, with the largest gains in few-shot/zero-shot and complex-constraint settings — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.08726)

### Inferences
- For the stated use case (forcing every number/name/date/citation marker from a short input to appear in a rewrite), the constraint type needed is simple positive lexical inclusion (not negation, not complex logical combinations), so the lighter-weight methods (Dynamic Beam Allocation, or Hugging Face's PhrasalConstraint/DisjunctiveConstraint, discussed below) are likely sufficient — the extra expressiveness and cost of full NeuroLogic CNF constraints or A*esque lookahead probably isn't necessary unless the facts have multiple acceptable surface forms (e.g., "3.2 million" vs "3,200,000") that are best expressed as disjunctive constraints per fact.
- All three method families are decoding-time only — they require no model retraining and are directly applicable to a small encoder-decoder's generate() loop, which fits the CPU-feasibility constraint well since they add only polynomial (for DBA, constant) overhead over plain beam search, not an additional model.

### Gaps
- No benchmark found comparing these methods specifically for a "preserve every number/name/date" constraint set (as opposed to MT/keyword-generation benchmarks); real-world overhead and failure rate for that exact use case would need direct experimentation.

## Are there existing libraries/implementations (Hugging Face `force_words_ids`, `DisjunctiveConstraint`, `PhrasalConstraint`, etc.) that could enforce required content at generation time without retraining? How mature/reliable is this tooling as of 2024-2025?

### Takeaway
Hugging Face `transformers` has (or had) first-class constrained beam search via `force_words_ids`/`constraints` (`PhrasalConstraint`, `DisjunctiveConstraint`) that is explicitly documented as working with encoder-decoder models including T5, and would directly let you enforce "every number/citation marker must appear" without any retraining — but as of the current (2025-era) `transformers` docs, this functionality appears to have been moved out of the core `generate()` path into an opt-in community "custom_generate" package, requiring `trust_remote_code=True`, which is a maturity/stability caveat to plan around.

### Cited Findings
- The current official `transformers` "Generation strategies" documentation page (fetched directly) lists only greedy search, sampling, and beam search under "Basic decoding methods," plus a generic "Custom generation methods" mechanism loaded via `custom_generate=<hub repo>` + `trust_remote_code=True`. It does **not** mention `force_words_ids`, `PhrasalConstraint`, or `DisjunctiveConstraint` as built-in core arguments anymore — [Generation strategies docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/en/generation_strategies)
- A dedicated community page, `transformers-community/constrained-beam-search`, now hosts this feature as a custom generation method. Usage: pass `custom_generate="transformers-community/constrained-beam-search"` plus `trust_remote_code=True` to `model.generate()`; specify required words/phrases via `force_words_ids` (nested lists express disjunctive "at least one of these options" constraints); constraint words must be tokenized with `add_special_tokens=False`; `do_sample` must be `False` since constrained decoding is incompatible with sampling; standard beam arguments (`num_beams`, `length_penalty`, `early_stopping`, `num_return_sequences`, `max_length`) still apply. The page explicitly lists compatibility with **"Encoder-decoder and decoder-only transformer models"** and its worked example loads `t5-base` via `AutoModelForSeq2SeqLM` and forces the German word "Sie" in a translation task — [transformers-community/constrained-beam-search](https://huggingface.co/transformers-community/constrained-beam-search)
- A merged `transformers` PR (#29163, "GenerationConfig validate both constraints and force_words_ids," merged ~Feb 2024, authored by FredericOdermatt, reviewed by gante/ArthurZucker) shows the `constraints`/`force_words_ids` arguments were still part of core `GenerationConfig` validation logic at least as of early 2024 — [PR diff](https://app.semanticdiff.com/gh/huggingface/transformers/pull/29163/overview)
- Community forum reports flag reliability issues even when the constraint API is used correctly: one user found forced words tended to land at the very start or end of GPT-2 output rather than mid-sentence, speculating the search pushes constraint tokens to the edges to preserve overall sequence probability — [HF forum thread](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/generate-constraint-words-within-the-output-sentence-and-not-at-its-start-end/23199); another user reported that with multiple constraints in a Donut document-classification setup, `force_words_ids` correctly resolved into the expected `PhrasalConstraint`/`DisjunctiveConstraint` objects internally, yet the output still looked wrong — treated as an anecdote/possible bug, not confirmed — [HF forum thread](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/constrain-output-format-from-beam-search-in-donut-doc-classification/23822)
- A broader survey on constrained generation (arXiv:2206.05395, "Why is constrained neural language generation particularly challenging?") notes these methods "force specific words into the output at every timestep, with no prior examination of those words," which can hurt output quality/naturalness, and that many methods "significantly impair task accuracy if they do not align the model's subword vocabulary with the external constraints" — e.g., a dynamic finite-state-automaton approach may accept "Hong Kong" as a unit but reject the separately-tokenized pieces "Hong" and "Kong," meaning multi-token facts (e.g., "3.2 million", "[12]") need careful tokenization-aware constraint construction — [arXiv:2206.05395](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.05395)
- An awesome-list resource exists for tracking the broader constrained-decoding ecosystem for LLMs: [Awesome-LLM-Constrained-Decoding (GitHub)](https://github.com/Saibo-creator/Awesome-LLM-Constrained-Decoding)

### Inferences
- The functional capability clearly exists and is documented to work with T5-class encoder-decoders — the open question is packaging/stability, not feasibility. Pinning a specific `transformers` version (or vendoring the `custom_generate/generate.py` + `beam_constraints.py` files directly into the project, which were found at `transformers-community/constrained-beam-search/raw/main/custom_generate/...`) would avoid depending on upstream community-repo maintenance and the `trust_remote_code=True` requirement.
- Given the reported edge-placement and correctness anecdotes, any production use should validate post-hoc (e.g., regex-check that every required number/citation marker is actually present in the decoded string) rather than trusting the constraint mechanism blindly — treat it as "much more likely to include the fact" rather than "guaranteed and well-placed."
- For a small 100M-1B model, tokenization-aware constraint construction matters more, not less: smaller T5 variants still use the same SentencePiece vocabulary as larger T5s, so the same multi-token-span caveat (e.g., citation markers like "[12]" or numbers like "3.2 million" splitting into multiple subword pieces) applies and must be handled by constructing `force_words_ids` as the exact token-ID sequence the tokenizer produces for that string, not word-by-word.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm the exact `transformers` version/date at which `force_words_ids`/`constraints` were removed from the core `generate()` API (only that a Feb 2024 PR still treated them as core, and that the current docs page no longer mentions them at all) — worth checking the `transformers` CHANGELOG directly before depending on either path.
- No first-hand confirmation (ran code) of the community `constrained-beam-search` package actually succeeding end-to-end on a small FLAN-T5 checkpoint; the claims above are as documented by the HF page, not independently verified in this research pass.

## What is grammar-constrained decoding / structured generation (guidance, outlines, LMQL) and could it be repurposed to constrain a rewrite task's output format or required-content inclusion?

### Takeaway
Grammar-constrained decoding tools (Guidance, Outlines, LMQL, SynCode, XGrammar) mask invalid tokens at each decoding step so outputs are valid-by-construction against a regex/CFG/JSON-schema — this is well suited to constraining *output format* (e.g., forcing a citation-marker syntax) but these tools are documented and benchmarked almost exclusively on decoder-only causal LMs; no source found confirms or denies seq2seq/encoder-decoder (T5-style) support, so this should be treated as an open compatibility question requiring direct testing, not an assumed yes.

### Cited Findings
- Core mechanism, per the SynCode paper's comparison (arXiv:2403.01632): "Constrained decoding intervenes in the decoding process of LMs by masking out invalid tokens based on given constraints and prefix tokens," so outputs are valid by construction rather than filtered after generation — [SynCode](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.01632)
- Library differences (same source): LMQL supports regex constraints but not context-free-grammar (CFG) constraints; Guidance supports CFG-guided generation via Earley parsing, but "incurs high inference overhead as it checks the syntactical validity of the entire model vocabulary at each step"; Outlines handles both regex and CFG constraints and uses Lark EBNF as its grammar input format — [SynCode / JSONSchemaBench summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.01632)
- JSONSchemaBench (arXiv:2501.10868) benchmarked six structured-output frameworks (including Guidance, Outlines, llama.cpp, XGrammar) and reports Guidance performing best on efficiency, schema coverage, and effect on task quality (noting the Guidance team was itself involved in that paper, a potential conflict of interest to flag) — [JSONSchemaBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.10868)
- A practitioner write-up reports Outlines supports JSON schemas, Pydantic models, regex, and EBNF grammars, integrates directly with Hugging Face Transformers as a logits processor, but has a practical weakness: grammar compilation time, with complex schemas taking "3-12 seconds to compile on first use" (a blog-reported figure, not a formal benchmark) — [Grammar-Constrained Generation blog post](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-16-grammar-constrained-generation-output-reliability)
- For serving engines: vLLM exposes `guided_json`, `guided_grammar`, `guided_regex` request parameters and auto-selects between XGrammar and Outlines backends; llama.cpp uses GBNF grammars. None of these serving-engine docs mention encoder-decoder/seq2seq models — [vLLM Outlines backend docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/v0.10.0/api/vllm/model_executor/guided_decoding/outlines_decoding.html); [vLLM Guidance backend docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/v0.9.1/api/vllm/model_executor/guided_decoding/guidance_decoding.html)
- A targeted search for Outlines/Guidance + T5/seq2seq/encoder-decoder support turned up no documentation either confirming or denying compatibility; all example code and docs found describe single-model (decoder-only) generation loops — search performed, no affirmative source found

### Inferences
- Because Outlines' and Guidance's Hugging Face Transformers integrations work as standard `LogitsProcessor` objects (a mechanism `generate()` applies uniformly regardless of architecture), they are *plausibly* usable with `AutoModelForSeq2SeqLM`/T5 since logits processors operate on the decoder's next-token distribution at each step regardless of whether that decoder is autoregressive-only or cross-attending to an encoder — but this is architectural inference, not a confirmed fact, since the library docs/examples found never demonstrate it.
- For this project's stated goal (force citation markers and numbers to appear, not necessarily constrain full output grammar), grammar-constrained decoding is probably over-engineered relative to simple lexical-constraint tooling (HF constrained beam search, NeuroLogic-style methods) — it's a better fit if you also want to enforce an output *format* (e.g., "rewrite must be valid JSON with a `facts` array") rather than just content inclusion.
- Guidance's documented high inference overhead (checking full-vocabulary syntactic validity at every step) is a real CPU-feasibility concern for a small model where decoding speed is already constrained; Outlines' one-time grammar compilation cost (seconds) is likely more tolerable since it's amortized across a batch/session rather than paid per-token.

### Gaps
- No source confirms grammar-constrained decoding has been run against a T5/FLAN-T5/BART encoder-decoder in practice. This would need direct experimentation (e.g., trying Outlines' `outlines.generate` against a `AutoModelForSeq2SeqLM` instance) to resolve before relying on it.
- No comparison found between grammar-constrained decoding and simple lexical constraint methods (HF `force_words_ids` / NeuroLogic) specifically for the "preserve required facts" use case — the two families target different problems (format validity vs. content inclusion) and their relative cost/benefit for fact-preservation specifically is unestablished in the literature found.

## Known failure modes/limitations of constrained decoding itself relevant to a small CPU-only model

### Takeaway
Constrained decoding reliably documents four recurring costs — reduced output fluency, a shrunken/distorted search space, slower decoding that scales with constraint count (for some algorithms), and "constraint satisfied but still wrong" outputs — all of which are more consequential for a small CPU-bound model than for a large GPU-served one, since the model has less fluency headroom to sacrifice and less compute budget to spend on search overhead.

### Cited Findings
- Fluency loss: forcing a phrase into the output "can clash with the surrounding text... we force the output to contain a phrase that may not be in agreement with the rest of the output," reported to be especially pronounced in morphologically rich languages — [End-to-End Lexically Constrained MT for Morphologically Rich Languages, arXiv:2106.12398](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.12398)
- A review states these beam methods "significantly slow down the decoding speed and tend to reduce the fluency of translation" — [cited via search synthesis, source: arXiv:2206.05395 family](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.05395)
- Restricted search space: forcing constraints into the output "dramatically limit[s] the search space of beam search especially with long phrase constraints," degrading final output quality — [Disambiguated Lexically Constrained NMT, arXiv:2305.17351](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.17351)
- Cost scaling: Grid Beam Search's decoding complexity is "linear to the number of constraints with effective beam size of k × (C+1)"; Dynamic Beam Allocation keeps cost closer to normal beam search but Post & Vilar still note it "requires a large beam size which is indirectly proportional to the number of constraints" in some framings before their O(1)-in-constraints result; a separate paper notes "slower inference compared to unmodified beam search" was a shortcoming these methods share, with vectorized beam allocation "in large part" solving it — [synthesis of Hokamp & Liu / Post & Vilar, see above citations]
- "Constraint satisfied but still wrong": in one translation comparison, Dynamic Beam Allocation correctly placed the required phrase but the output also contained an extra, incorrect target word, and omitted the relevant source phrase elsewhere — i.e., satisfying the lexical constraint did not guarantee overall output correctness — [search synthesis referencing DBA evaluation examples]
- Enforcement-mechanism subtlety: some methods only enforce constraints when the model's attention/alignment happens to land on the constrained source span ("a passive method enforces constraints only when the prior alignment is at a constrained source span"); posterior-alignment approaches that revise tokens after scoring can misrepresent both the alignment and the token probabilities — [Accurate Online Posterior Alignments for Principled Lexically-Constrained Decoding, arXiv:2204.00871](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.00871)
- Non-decoding alternatives (e.g., data-side placeholders or appending target constraints to the input) "do not guarantee the appearance of the target constraints in some cases" either — constrained decoding is not uniquely flawed; the alternative of just prompting/data-augmentation is not a safe fallback — [synthesis, same search cluster]
- General survey point: constrained decoding methods "force specific words into the output at every timestep, with no prior examination of those words," which can hurt naturalness, and performance degrades further if the model's subword vocabulary isn't aligned with the external constraint tokens — [Why is constrained neural language generation particularly challenging?, arXiv:2206.05395](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.05395)

### Inferences
- Most of the hard evidence for these failure modes comes from the machine-translation literature (long phrase constraints, morphologically rich target languages), which is a more adversarial setting than inserting short, already-correctly-formed spans (numbers, names, citation markers) into a same-language rewrite — the severity of fluency/search-space degradation for this project's specific use case (single-language rewrite, short atomic fact-spans as constraints) is likely to be less severe than the MT numbers suggest, but this is an inference, not a measured result.
- For a CPU-only small model, Dynamic Beam Allocation's O(1)-in-constraints cost profile is the most favorable of the beam-search-family methods if the number of required facts per input is non-trivial (e.g., more than 3-4 numbers/names/citations); Grid Beam Search's linear-in-constraints cost could become a bottleneck if a short input densely packs many facts.
- Given the "constraint satisfied but still wrong" failure mode, constrained decoding should be treated as a floor-raising technique (fewer dropped facts) rather than a complete faithfulness guarantee — a post-hoc faithfulness check (e.g., regex/string match of required facts against the decoded output, as HF's own tooling doesn't guarantee correctness of placement) remains necessary regardless of which constrained-decoding method is used.

### Gaps
- No source found measuring constrained-decoding overhead or fluency degradation specifically on CPU inference or at the ~100M-1B parameter scale; all cited cost/fluency figures come from GPU-trained MT systems of unspecified (likely larger) scale.

## Is there research on applying copy mechanisms / constrained decoding specifically to small or distilled models rather than large ones?

### Takeaway
There is an emerging but thin 2024-2025 literature on faithfulness specifically for small LLMs (sub-4B parameters), converging on two main strategies — plan-guided generation and preference-optimization with synthetic hallucination injection — rather than copy mechanisms or constrained decoding per se; no source found directly combines copy mechanisms or lexically-constrained decoding with small-model distillation, leaving that combination as a research gap your project would be filling rather than replicating.

### Cited Findings
- "Exploration of Plan-Guided Summarization for Narrative Texts: the Case of Small Language Models" (arXiv:2504.09071, 2025): explicitly frames the problem — "small language models of less than 4 billion parameters are prone to 'hallucinations,' where names, dates, or claims in a summary don't match the source." Its mitigation is to ground summaries in an extracted plan reflecting relevant source material, rather than a copy mechanism or constrained decoding — [arXiv:2504.09071](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.09071)
- TrueBrief (arXiv:2601.04212, submitted Dec 2025): a framework "designed to enhance the faithfulness of small LLMs (SLMs) primarily for the task of text summarization through a preference-optimization paradigm," using a data-generation module that does "controlled hallucination injection to generate synthetic preference data." This is a training-time (DPO-style) approach, not a decoding-time constraint — [arXiv:2601.04212](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.04212); [HTML version](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.04212v1)
- FaithBench (Oct 2024): a summarization-hallucination benchmark finding GPT-4o/GPT-3.5-Turbo produce the least hallucinations among the models tested, and that even the best hallucination-detection models achieve near-50% accuracy on the benchmark overall — this benchmark's model coverage skews toward larger models, so it does not directly answer the small-model question, but it does establish that even detecting unfaithfulness (let alone preventing it) remains an open, hard problem across model scales — [referenced via search synthesis, FaithBench Oct 2024]
- No paper found that applies NeuroLogic Decoding, Grid Beam Search, Dynamic Beam Allocation, or HF-style `force_words_ids` constraints specifically within a small/distilled-model faithful-summarization pipeline. Likewise, no paper found that retrofits a copy mechanism onto a model smaller than PEGASUS-base-scale specifically for a faithfulness motivation at the sub-1B/CPU-feasible regime.

### Inferences
- The absence of literature combining (a) small/distilled model scale with (b) copy mechanisms or constrained decoding specifically (as opposed to plan-guidance or preference optimization) suggests this project's proposed approach is relatively novel rather than well-trodden — which cuts both ways: less risk of simply re-deriving a known negative result, but also no existing benchmark or ablation to calibrate expected gains against before implementing.
- Because constrained decoding and copy mechanisms are both decoding/architecture-level (not scale-dependent) techniques, their core mechanics (beam masking, pointer mixing weights) do not inherently require a large model to function — the open risk is specifically whether a small model's weaker language-modeling fluency makes it more sensitive to the fluency-degradation failure mode documented in the constrained-decoding literature above, which is an empirical question this research did not find pre-answered.

### Gaps
- No existing ablation study found that isolates "does constrained decoding hurt fluency more on a small model than a large one" — this is likely something to measure empirically rather than look up.
- No existing work found that directly compares copy-mechanism retrofitting vs. constrained decoding vs. plan-guided generation vs. preference optimization head-to-head for small-model faithfulness; the four approaches currently sit in disconnected literatures (classic copy-mechanism papers, classic/NLP-adjacent constrained-decoding papers, and brand-new 2024-2025 small-LLM-faithfulness papers) rather than a unified comparison.
