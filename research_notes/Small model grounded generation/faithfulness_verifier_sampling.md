# Faithfulness Evaluation and Best-of-N / Generate-then-Verify Sampling for Small Models

## How is faithfulness measured/detected in generated text (FactCC, SummaC, QAGS, and similar)? Which are classifier-, QA-, or NLI-based, and which are practical to run locally/cheaply?

### Takeaway
There are three families of automatic faithfulness metrics — classifier-based (FactCC), NLI/entailment-based (SummaC and generic NLI cross-encoders), and QA-based (QAGS, QAFactEval) — and the NLI-entailment family is the most practical to run locally/cheaply on CPU with a small model, since it needs only a BERT/ALBERT-scale cross-encoder rather than an LLM or API call. All three families are **detection** tools (a gate), not generation techniques.

### Cited Findings
- **FactCC** (Kryściński et al., EMNLP 2020) is a weakly-supervised, model-based binary classifier (fine-tuned BERT) trained on synthetically generated consistent/inconsistent sentence pairs created via rule-based transformations of source sentences (semantically invariant edits like back-translation vs. semantically variant edits like negation, entity/number/pronoun swaps). It is trained jointly on three objectives: consistency classification, extracting a supporting span from the source, and extracting the inconsistent span from the summary. It substantially outperformed prior NLI/fact-checking models trained with strong supervision when applied to real summarization system outputs. — [Evaluating the Factual Consistency of Abstractive Text Summarization (ACL Anthology)](https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.750); [FactCC GitHub](https://github.com/salesforce/factCC/blob/master/README.md)
- **SummaC** (Laban et al., TACL 2022) revisits NLI models for inconsistency detection. Prior NLI-based approaches had performed poorly because of a granularity mismatch — NLI datasets are sentence-level, but consistency checking is done over whole documents. SummaC's fix (SummaCConv) splits documents into sentences and aggregates NLI scores across sentence pairs. SummaCConv reaches 74.4% balanced accuracy on the SummaC benchmark (6 standardized datasets), ~5 points above prior work. The package also ships a zero-shot variant, SummaC-ZS, that needs no additional training — just an off-the-shelf NLI model and a sentence-splitting/aggregation scheme. — [SummaC paper (ACL Anthology / TACL)](https://aclanthology.org/2022.tacl-1.10); [SummaC code/PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/summac/0.0.2)
- **QAGS** (Wang, Cho, Lewis) is QA-based: it generates questions from the summary, answers them against both the summary and the source, and flags inconsistency when answers disagree. It correlates better with human judgments than other automatic metrics of its era and produces token-level explanations (which questions/tokens are inconsistent). — [Asking and Answering Questions to Evaluate Factual Consistency of Summaries](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2004.04228)
- **QAFactEval** (a later QA-based metric) improves on QAGS-style metrics by ~14% average on the SummaC benchmark and also beats the best entailment-based metric; the paper finds QA-based and entailment-based (NLI) signals are complementary and can be combined for a further boost. — [QAFactEval paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.08542)
- A taxonomy from a 2021 survey classifies these metrics as sentence-level, entity-level, or token-level, with FactCC as a sentence-level classifier and QAGS/entailment metrics covering other granularities. — [The Factual Inconsistency Problem in Abstractive Text Summarization: A Survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.14839)
- **Practical/cheap local options**: Compact cross-encoder NLI models are the cheapest to run. "Provenance" is a lightweight RAG fact-checker using a compact open-source NLI cross-encoder (from Vectara) rather than an LLM, explicitly to keep latency/cost low and avoid any fine-tuning. — [Provenance paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.01022)
- **MiniTrue** (from the PrefixNLI paper) is a lightweight entailment model that performs comparably to TrueTeacher on the TRUE summarization benchmark despite being significantly smaller — a concrete example of a small, locally-runnable entailment checker. — [PrefixNLI paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.01359)
- Older but concrete CPU latency numbers: WikiCheck (2021) reports bert-base-uncased at ~0.1s/sample on CPU and albert-base at ~0.08s/sample (vs. ~0.006s on GPU), with ~85% SNLI accuracy for these base-size NLI models. These numbers are from older hardware and should be treated as rough/dated. — [WikiCheck paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.00835)
- A 2025 study found that six widely-used reference-free factuality metrics (originally built for short-form summarization) are unreliable when evaluated on long documents — a caution against assuming these metrics transfer to all input lengths/domains without validation. — [noted in QAFactEval-adjacent search results, arxiv.org/pdf/2511.07689v1]

### Inferences
- For a CPU-only, small-model pipeline, a SummaC-ZS-style zero-shot NLI cross-encoder (sentence-split + aggregate) or a MiniTrue-scale compact entailment model is the most practical faithfulness gate: no fine-tuning required, sub-second-per-sentence CPU latency is achievable with base/ALBERT-scale models, and it directly matches what the user's system already does (a fidelity gate).
- FactCC-style classifiers require either using the released pretrained weights as-is or fine-tuning on synthetic data; they are heavier to stand up from scratch than a generic NLI cross-encoder but are available pretrained.
- QA-based metrics (QAGS, QAFactEval) are generally more expensive (they require a QA model plus a question-generation model, i.e., two extra models/passes) and are less obviously "cheap to run locally" than a single NLI cross-encoder forward pass.
- All of FactCC/SummaC/QAGS are detection methods used as a scorer/filter; none of them, by themselves, produce more faithful text — they only grade candidates already generated. This matches exactly what the user's system already has as "a gate."

### Gaps
- Exact current SOTA accuracy numbers for SummaC-ZS specifically (vs. SummaCConv) were not retrieved from primary sources in this session.
- No controlled, apples-to-apples benchmark of inference latency/accuracy for FactCC vs. SummaC vs. QAFactEval on identical hardware was found.

## What is "faithfulness-aware decoding" — are there decoding-time techniques that rerank/filter multiple sampled generations by a faithfulness score, distinct from constrained decoding?

### Takeaway
Yes — this is a well-documented, named line of work. The central paper (Wan et al., EACL 2023, "Faithfulness-Aware Decoding Strategies for Abstractive Summarization") proposes exactly "generate multiple candidates via beam search, then rerank by a faithfulness score" (plus a more expensive lookahead variant), and reports it works even on small (~400-568M parameter) seq2seq models — directly relevant to the user's scenario. This is a generation-side (not just detection-side) technique, though it still requires an initial candidate pool that contains a faithful option.

### Cited Findings
- **Wan et al. (EACL 2023)** propose two decoding-time methods on top of standard beam search with BART-large (406M params) and PEGASUS-Large (568M params) on XSum and CNN/DM:
  - **Ranking**: generate beam-search candidates, score each with a composite faithfulness metric (weighted combination of FactCC, BS-Fact, DAE, and QuestEval, weights fit by linear regression against human faithfulness judgments), and select the top-scoring candidate.
  - **Lookahead**: at each decoding step, add a reference-free faithfulness score — computed on a full greedy continuation from the current prefix — to the token's model score, evaluated over only the top-5 candidate tokens per step (to bound cost).
  - **Combined** (Beam+Lookahead+Ranking): lookahead improves the beam candidates, which are then reranked.
  - On CNN/DM with BART-large, beam search (k=10) scored BS-Fact 94.20 / FactCC 84.23 / DAE 3.30 (lower better) / QuestEval 60.03, versus nucleus sampling (p=0.9) at BS-Fact 91.76 / FactCC 54.05 / DAE 21.61 / QuestEval 56.43 — beam search was markedly more faithful on every metric, especially FactCC (84 vs 54) and DAE error rate (3.3 vs 21.6). On XSum, beam search also beat nucleus sampling (DAE error 63.49 vs 76.20).
  - Varying beam size 2→10 raised the *best possible* (oracle-max) faithfulness score in the pool steadily, but the *selected* (ranked top-1) score improved only marginally — meaning the faithful candidate exists in a larger pool, but the ranking/selection signal is the bottleneck, not pool size alone.
  - A distillation step trains a student model so that plain greedy decoding approximates the gains of ranking/lookahead, avoiding extra inference cost at deployment. — [Faithfulness-Aware Decoding Strategies for Abstractive Summarization, ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.03278); [ACL Anthology version](https://aclanthology.org/2023.eacl-main.210)
- **Falke et al. (2019)**, a precursor, used NLI models to rerank summarization candidates to reduce unfaithful errors; Wan et al. note NLI model quality was a limiting factor for this earlier approach. — [cited via Wan et al. search results]
- A 2022 paper adds NLI-assisted beam reranking during "saliency-enhanced greedy decoding," scoring entailment between the input and each beam candidate, evaluated on XSum and CNN/DM. — [ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.02712]
- **PrefixNLI (2025)** identifies a mismatch: NLI models are trained on complete sentences but decoding must score partial prefixes. It trains MiniTruePrefixes, beating baseline NLI models by 5-14 F1 points at prefix-level entailment; when used to guide decoding, a 3B model matched the faithfulness/runtime of an 8B model at half the memory — a technique for producing (not just detecting) more faithful output cheaply, though built for larger decoder-only LLMs rather than the <1B seq2seq regime. — [PrefixNLI paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.01359)
- **Composition Sampling** (a diverse-decoding alternative) found nucleus sampling gives the most diverse summaries but poor faithfulness; a constrained "composition" sampling variant gets both diversity and the best entailment scores on XSum/CNN-DM, confirmed by human eval. — [A Well-Composed Text is Half Done! (arxiv.org/pdf/2203.15108)]
- **ConSUM** (2026 preprint) reranks candidates by consistency with the source *and* consensus among the other candidates via Minimum Bayes Risk decoding — i.e., combining a faithfulness reranker with self-consistency-style agreement. Only the abstract was available; results unconfirmed. — [papers.cool/arxiv/2605.29336]
- **Contrastive decoding approaches** are a related but distinct technique family (not reranking a sampled pool, but modifying the per-token distribution at decode time):
  - **Context-aware decoding (CAD)**: contrasts next-token probabilities with vs. without the source document, upweighting tokens whose probability rises when context is present. Reports 21% ROUGE-L and 14.3% factuality improvement for LLaMA-30B on CNN-DM. — [CAD paper (NAACL 2024)](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-short.69)
  - **DeCoRe**: contrasts base-LLM output against a version with "retrieval heads" masked, using conditional entropy as a guide; reports 18.6% improvement on XSum. — [DeCoRe, Findings EMNLP 2025]
  - **FECS (Fidelity-Enriched Contrastive Search)**: extends contrastive search decoding with source-similarity promotion and repetition penalty; reports faithfulness gains "across various language model sizes" while keeping diversity comparable to standard decoding. — [FECS paper (ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.14981)]

### Inferences
- The Wan et al. result is the single most directly relevant piece of evidence for the user's exact scenario: a ~400-500M parameter seq2seq model, reranking multiple beam candidates by an automatic faithfulness score, measurably improves faithfulness over picking one decoding path (beam) or over plain sampling (nucleus). This supports "generate N candidates, keep the one that passes a fidelity gate" as a documented and empirically validated technique at this model scale.
- However, Wan et al.'s own finding that oracle-max faithfulness rises with pool size while *selected* faithfulness rises only marginally is an important caveat: the bottleneck isn't necessarily "not enough samples," it's "selection signal quality." A good gate (accurate NLI/entailment check) matters more than cranking up N.
- Nucleus/temperature sampling specifically underperforms beam search for faithfulness in the one directly-comparable study found (Wan et al.); this cuts against the assumption that stochastic sampling is the right way to generate the diverse candidate pool for a best-of-N fidelity-gate scheme. Diverse beam search or constrained "composition" sampling look like better levers for diversity-without-faithfulness-loss than plain temperature/nucleus sampling.
- Contrastive decoding methods are a different lane: they reshape the per-token distribution of a *single* generation rather than filtering a sampled set, so they are a complementary technique to best-of-N+verifier, not a substitute, and the published results are mostly on larger (7B+) LLMs, not confirmed for <1B seq2seq models.

### Gaps
- No study was found that applies Wan-et-al.-style ranking/lookahead specifically to a <1B-parameter model doing a *narrow rewriting* task (as opposed to open-ended summarization on XSum/CNN-DM) — the user's exact use case is adjacent but not identical.
- No controlled comparison of "beam+rerank" vs. "high-temperature sampling+rerank" vs. "diverse-beam+rerank" specifically isolating the sampling-strategy variable under a fixed verifier was found.

## What does research say about best-of-N / rejection sampling with a verifier as a technique to improve small models' reliability without fine-tuning? Is there evidence for content-preservation/faithfulness specifically, not just reasoning benchmarks?

### Takeaway
Best-of-N/rejection-sampling-with-a-verifier is a well-documented test-time-scaling technique, but almost all of the rigorous quantitative evidence (ROC-curve analysis, scaling laws, verifier-imperfection studies) comes from math/reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH500) on models ≥0.6B-32B, not from faithfulness/content-preservation tasks specifically. The faithfulness-specific evidence that exists (Wan et al., reranking beam candidates by a faithfulness metric) supports the same qualitative conclusion — reranking a sampled pool by a verifier improves outcomes — but was not run as a formal best-of-N/rejection-sampling scaling study and not on sub-1B models.

### Cited Findings
- **Self-consistency** (Wang et al., original paper) samples multiple chain-of-thought reasoning paths and takes a majority vote on the final answer; it requires no fine-tuning, no auxiliary model, and works off-the-shelf, typically sampling 5-40 times. Reported gains vary hugely by task: ~18 points on GSM8K but only ~3.5 points on ARC-Challenge, and simple unweighted majority vote performed as well as probability-weighted voting (the model assigns similar probability to all its own generated paths). — [Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning (search synthesis); arxiv.org/pdf/2203.11171]
- Voting-based self-consistency only works when answers are comparable/countable (numbers, multiple-choice letters); open-ended text needs a different aggregation mechanism — directly relevant since faithfulness-checked rewriting is open-ended, not multiple-choice. — [search synthesis, self-consistency sources]
- Self-consistency/voting reduces variance, not bias: it cannot fix a systematic/deterministic error shared across all samples. — [search synthesis, self-consistency sources]
- **ROC-n-reroll (Dorner et al.)**: Best-of-N and rejection-sampling accuracy is precisely characterized by the generator's initial accuracy and the verifier's ROC curve. On GSM8K/MATH500 with Qwen3 models, rejection sampling (RS) outperforms best-of-N (BoN) at matched compute when the ROC curve is concave; both converge to the same accuracy ceiling at infinite compute (a shared ceiling set by verifier imperfection); and low-compute performance does not reliably predict high-compute performance — two verifiers performing nearly identically at low N can diverge sharply at high N. — [liner.com summary of arXiv paper; cf. arxiv.org/pdf/2510.04087v2]
- **Backtracking tokenwise rejection sampling** (Li et al., "On the Query Complexity of Verifier-Assisted Language Generation"): allowing the sampler to erase/backtrack the last few tokens gives robust efficiency, accuracy, and diversity gains over blockwise rejection sampling and nucleus sampling baselines, tested with CodeLlama on test-case generation — a constrained-generation (not pure reasoning) task, though not faithfulness/summarization specifically. — [arxiv.org/pdf/2502.12123]
- **"Best of mini-N in-loop Sampling" (Rho & Lee)**: plain best-of-N becomes unreliable for hard prompts as N grows, because the probability of "false acceptance" (accepting the least-bad candidate that still fails a quality threshold) rises with N; the proposed fix splits budget into smaller loops with early exit and a reward model trained with an explicit "reject/outside option." — [arxiv.org/html/2507.12399v2, search synthesis]
- No direct best-of-N-for-faithfulness scaling study (analogous to the GSM8K ROC-curve work) was found; the closest faithfulness-specific analogue is Wan et al.'s ranking method (see previous section), which is a form of best-of-N with a faithfulness verifier, applied to 406-568M-parameter BART/PEGASUS models, and which does show improvement over single-path beam decoding.

### Inferences
- The combination of these findings supports the user's proposed technique *directionally*: best-of-N + verifier is a real, validated pattern for improving reliability without fine-tuning, and the one faithfulness-specific instance of it (Wan et al.) that exists at small-model scale shows a measurable gain.
- But the reasoning-benchmark literature's core warning transfers directly: a best-of-N+verifier scheme has an accuracy ceiling set by the verifier's own error rate (not by N), and increasing N without a good verifier risks false acceptance of a bad candidate, not better output. For the user's system, the practical implication is that validating/characterizing the NLI-gate's own false-negative rate (how often it passes an actually-unfaithful candidate) matters as much as choosing N.
- Self-consistency per se (majority vote) is a poor fit for the user's exact task, since faithfulness-checked rewriting output isn't naturally reducible to a countable answer; the "generate N, keep first that passes a gate" scheme is closer to rejection sampling / best-of-N with a binary verifier than to self-consistency voting, and that framing has more direct supporting evidence (Wan et al.; ROC-n-reroll).

### Gaps
- No evidence was found of best-of-N/rejection-sampling specifically on a <1B-parameter model for a content-preservation/faithfulness task with a formal scaling analysis (how accuracy changes with N). This is a direct gap relative to the user's objective and is the single biggest unanswered empirical question in this research.
- No study explicitly combining "small seq2seq model (<1B)" + "NLI verifier" + "formal best-of-N scaling curve" was found; this would need to be benchmarked by the user directly if it matters precisely.

## Is there research quantifying how many samples (N) are typically needed before at least one faithful candidate appears, for small (<1B) models on constrained rewriting/summarization?

### Takeaway
No research was found that directly quantifies N-for-faithfulness on sub-1B constrained rewriting/summarization models. The closest adjacent evidence is from code-generation pass@k studies (different task, different model sizes) and Wan et al.'s beam-size sweep (2-10), which showed oracle-best faithfulness in the candidate pool rises steadily with pool size but the *selected* top candidate only marginally improves — implying pool size alone is not the limiting factor once you're already in the 2-10 range.

### Cited Findings
- Wan et al. varied beam size 2→10 on BART-large/PEGASUS-large and found the maximum-possible (oracle) faithfulness score within the candidate pool rose steadily with beam size, but the actual selected (top-ranked) score only marginally improved — the bottleneck was the ranking/selection signal, not the number of candidates. — [ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.03278]
- In code generation (different domain, not faithfulness), Codex-style pass@k studies draw up to 200 candidates per problem to reliably estimate pass@1/10/100 with an unbiased estimator; the HuggingFace HumanEval demo commonly uses pass@1/10/20. Optimal temperature rises with k: near-0 temperature is best for pass@1, and temperature near 1 is best for pass@100. Constrained-generation work sometimes uses as few as 3 samples at temperature 0.7 for pass@1/2/3. — [HumanEval / Codex paper summary, arxiv.org/pdf/2107.03374]
- No N-vs-faithfulness curve for any seq2seq/rewriting model, small or large, was found in this session's searches.

### Inferences
- Given Wan et al.'s finding that selection quality (not pool size) is the bottleneck in the 2-10 candidate range, it's plausible the user's system would see diminishing returns from N well before N=10, provided it uses a reasonably accurate gate — but this is an inference, not a measured result, and may not transfer from beam-search candidate pools to independently-sampled (temperature/nucleus) pools.
- The absence of any small-model, faithfulness-specific N-sweep study means the user would likely need to run their own small sweep (N = 1, 2, 4, 8…) against their own gate and task to get a reliable answer — this is explicitly a research gap, not something to take on faith from the literature.

### Gaps
- No paper was found reporting an N-required-for-faithfulness curve on any summarization/rewriting model. This is the most significant unanswered question relative to the user's stated objective, and the user should treat it as something to measure empirically rather than look up.

## Known failure modes of best-of-N + verifier (verifier wrong; systematic/deterministic model bias vs. random variance)

### Takeaway
Both failure modes the user asked about are independently documented: (1) verifier imperfection sets an accuracy ceiling and can cause "false acceptance" that gets worse, not better, as N grows; and (2) when a model's error is systematic/model-level (not a search/sampling artifact), more candidates mostly reproduce the same biased output, so best-of-N provides little or no benefit — this is a critical distinction for whether the technique will help the user's small model at all, since it depends on whether the model's failure is better characterized as stochastic noise or a systematic bias.

### Cited Findings
- **Verifier sets a ceiling, not a floor.** Best-of-N and rejection-sampling accuracy is governed by the generator's accuracy and the verifier's ROC curve; both methods converge to the *same* ceiling at high compute, meaning no amount of extra sampling gets past an imperfect verifier's limitations. — [ROC-n-reroll / Dorner et al. summary]
- **PRM false positives impose an asymptotic ceiling on best-of-N performance** — i.e., if the verifier systematically scores a wrong/unfaithful candidate as good, sampling more candidates cannot overcome that error; it just makes the false positive more likely to be selected. — [More Test-Time Compute Can Hurt: Overestimation Bias in LLM Beam Search, arxiv.org/abs/2603.15377, and cited related work]
- **False acceptance probability rises with N.** As N grows, the probability of accepting the "least-bad" candidate that still fails a quality threshold increases — i.e., a weak verifier gets worse (more likely to pass bad output), not just unhelpful, as more candidates are drawn, unless paired with an explicit reject/threshold option. — [Best of mini-N in-loop Sampling, arxiv.org/html/2507.12399v2]
- **Model-level bias vs. search-level error.** A study of beam search for open-ended generation found degenerate beam-search outputs are "mainly attributed to model errors instead of search errors" — i.e., the trained model itself assigns a higher score to a degenerate/wrong output, so widening search (or by extension increasing N) mostly surfaces more of the same biased outputs rather than fixing the problem, because better search algorithms are "unlikely to help" when the error is in the model's own distribution. — [Investigating Label Bias in Beam Search for Open-ended Text Generation, arxiv.org/pdf/2005.11009]
- **Beam selection over a noisy scorer introduces systematic overestimation bias that grows with pool size**, and beyond a "critical width" specific to the scorer, search/sampling degrades rather than improves results; a low-noise scorer (e.g., a reward/process model) has a much larger critical width (useful gains up to 8.9 points) than a high-noise scorer (perplexity), which has essentially zero useful width. — [More Test-Time Compute Can Hurt, arxiv.org/abs/2603.15377]
- **Diversity alone is not sufficient** — in a machine-translation gender-bias study, beam search was shown to amplify demographic biases; the fix required both explicitly diversifying the n-best list *and* reranking with an independent signal (not the model's own likelihood) — illustrating that fixing a systematic bias needs an intervention beyond "just sample more," matching the "systematic/deterministic" failure mode the user is worried about. — [cited gender-bias-in-beam-search work, search synthesis]

### Inferences
- These findings directly bear on the user's framing: if their small model's unreliable output is a **systematic/deterministic** bias (e.g., always drops a specific type of detail, or beam search reliably converges on a particular unfaithful phrasing because the *model itself* scores that phrasing highly), then "generate N candidates and keep the first that passes a gate" is unlikely to work well, because all or most of the N candidates may share the same defect, especially under beam search or low-temperature decoding — this matches the literature's "model error, not search error" framing.
- Conversely, if the unreliability is closer to **stochastic/random variance** (the model sometimes gets it right and sometimes doesn't, somewhat independently across samples), best-of-N + a reasonably accurate verifier should help, bounded by the verifier's own error rate (its ROC curve) as a ceiling.
- Practical implication: the user should check (a) whether varying decoding temperature/sampling actually produces *varied* outputs with respect to the specific fidelity failure they care about (if all samples fail identically, the bias is systematic and best-of-N won't help — switch to fixing the model/prompt/constrained-decoding instead), and (b) separately validate their NLI gate's own false-negative rate, since an inaccurate gate will asymptotically cap any gains from more sampling, and per the false-acceptance finding, may actively get worse (not just plateau) as N increases without an explicit reject threshold.

### Gaps
- No study was found that diagnoses, for a specific small generative model, whether a given failure is "systematic model bias" vs. "stochastic variance" — this is something the user would need to test directly (e.g., by checking whether N independent samples at nonzero temperature actually disagree on the failure in question).

## Is temperature/nucleus sampling documented to produce more diverse candidates more likely to include a faithful one, for small seq2seq models specifically?

### Takeaway
The one directly relevant study found (Wan et al., EACL 2023, BART-large/PEGASUS-large, 406-568M params) reports the opposite of the premise: nucleus sampling produced the *least* faithful summaries of the decoding strategies tested, while beam search (especially with larger beam width) produced the most faithful ones. Diverse-but-unfaithful candidate pools from nucleus sampling do not straightforwardly contain "a faithful one more often" — diversity and faithfulness pulled in different directions in this evidence.

### Cited Findings
- On CNN/DM with BART-large: nucleus sampling (p=0.9) scored FactCC 54.05 and DAE error rate 21.61, versus beam search (k=10) at FactCC 84.23 and DAE 3.30 — nucleus sampling was dramatically less faithful on both metrics, not more likely to surface a faithful candidate. — [Wan et al., ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.03278]
- Composition Sampling research corroborates this: nucleus sampling generates the most *diverse* summaries, but "these summaries perform poorly on faithfulness measures" — diversity from nucleus sampling specifically does not translate to more faithful candidates; a structured "composition sampling" variant was needed to get both diversity and faithfulness (best entailment scores on XSum/CNN-DM, confirmed by human eval). — [A Well-Composed Text is Half Done!, arxiv.org/pdf/2203.15108]
- A 2025-2026 paper on factual consistency notes beam search tends to yield higher factuality scores than other decoding strategies, "especially random sampling," and that temperature primarily controls output variance/diversity, not faithfulness directly. — [search synthesis, factual consistency preference-learning paper, arxiv.org/pdf/2605.26840]
- A recent study of LLM summarization (large models, not small seq2seq) found temperature changes had only small effects on summarization quality — but this is about large LLMs, not small seq2seq models, so it doesn't settle the small-model case either way. — [search synthesis]

### Inferences
- For the user's small-model scenario, this evidence argues *against* relying on temperature/nucleus sampling to generate the candidate pool for a best-of-N fidelity-gate scheme. A better-supported alternative (per the same literature) is to generate the pool via beam search (ideally a larger beam, or diverse beam search) and apply the faithfulness reranker/gate to beam candidates, rather than via stochastic sampling.
- This is a meaningful course-correction relative to the premise in the user's key questions: "more diverse via temperature sampling → more likely to include a faithful candidate" is not what the one small-model-scale study found; the opposite pattern (nucleus sampling = least faithful) was reported.

### Gaps
- No study using a sub-1B seq2seq model with a systematic temperature/top-p sweep (rather than a single nucleus-sampling setting vs. beam search) correlated against faithfulness was found. The Wan et al. comparison uses one nucleus setting (p=0.9) vs. beam — a finer-grained sweep (e.g., temperature 0.3 vs 0.7 vs 1.0) specifically for faithfulness was not located.
- No evidence was found isolating "sampling strategy" from "model size" — i.e., whether the nucleus-sampling faithfulness penalty shrinks or grows as model size decreases toward the user's own (likely smaller than 406M) model.
