# Small models drop facts by training, not by accident

A 77M-parameter flan-t5-small fails at "rewrite these five verified facts into one fluent paragraph" not because it is too small to understand the task, but because **every layer of its training stack — pretraining objective, instruction-tuning mixture, and decoding algorithm — independently rewards short, partial-coverage output over complete, multi-fact fusion**, and these biases compound rather than cancel. T5's span-corruption pretraining trains the decoder to predict only short "gaps," never to reproduce long stretches of given content; FLAN instruction-tuning layers a mostly short-answer task mixture on top of that; and beam search, the default decoding strategy, has been shown experimentally to assign its own *highest* probability to emptier, shorter outputs almost half the time, independent of search algorithm. Multi-document summarization research shows the same model class learns, from its training data, that one dominant source "covers" a summary well enough — so a handful of equally-weighted unique facts get triaged exactly like redundant source documents, with the non-dominant ones treated as optional. None of this is specific to flan-t5-small's checkpoint; it is baked into the T5 training recipe at every scale, so the failure should attenuate — not vanish — with model size. Four fix families exist in the literature (copy mechanisms, constrained decoding, faithfulness-aware reranking/best-of-N, and narrow fine-tuning on fusion-shaped data), each targeting a different layer of the problem, and the evidence favors narrow fine-tuning plus a cheap post-hoc verifier as the highest-leverage, CPU-feasible combination — not because the other techniques don't work, but because the core defect here is a training-distribution mismatch that decoding-time patches can only partially paper over. This is best understood as a genuinely under-addressed research gap for the specific shape of "combine N discrete, non-redundant facts, preserving all of them" rather than a solved problem being merely misapplied — close analogues exist (sentence fusion, data-to-text generation) but no source directly tests this exact task formulation at sub-1B scale.

## Three independent biases converge on the same failure

The clearest mechanistic story starts with what T5 was actually trained to do. T5's span-corruption pretraining objective corrupts contiguous spans of input and replaces each with a single sentinel token, so the decoder only ever has to regenerate the dropped spans — making its training targets far shorter than its inputs by design; one documented pretraining configuration pairs a 568-token input with just a 114-token target ([Cerebras T5 docs](https://docs.cerebras.net/en/latest/wsc/port/_autosummary/cerebras.modelzoo.data.nlp.t5.t5_utils.html)). Raffel et al. chose this specifically because it is "slightly more computationally efficient due to shorter target sequence lengths" ([Raffel et al. 2020, via HF forum](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/preprocessing-for-t5-denoising/6266)) — a compute decision with no regard for length fidelity. FLAN instruction-tuning then layers a large task mixture (1.8K tasks for the flagship model) dominated by classification, short-form QA, and extractive tasks ([Chung et al. 2022, arXiv 2210.11416](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11416v3)) on top of that short-target prior, rather than correcting it. The practical consequence is directly measurable: an empirical probe found FLAN-T5-small defaults to ~9.8-word outputs unprompted and is **largely insensitive to explicit length instructions** — producing essentially the same ~10-word output whether told to write 10 or 15 words, unlike decoder-only LLMs tested in the same study ([arXiv 2406.16356](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.16356)). A model with this little steerability over its own output length has no mechanism to "decide" to write a longer paragraph just because five facts were supplied; it defaults to the length its training distribution calibrated it to produce, and the cheapest way to hit that length is to select a subset of the input and discard the rest.

Decoding compounds this rather than merely inheriting it. Stahlberg and Byrne ran *exact* search (not just beam search) on a transformer NMT model and found that for **more than 50% of sentences, the model's own global-best-scoring output was the empty translation** — demonstrating the brevity bias lives in the model's learned probability landscape itself, not in beam search as a search heuristic ([Stahlberg & Byrne, EMNLP 2019](https://preview.aclanthology.org/moar-dois/D19-1331.pdf)). Holtzman et al. independently showed that *widening* the beam makes this worse, not better: with beam widths of 64+, GPT-2 models increasingly "prefer to stop generating immediately after the given context" ([Holtzman et al., ICLR 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.09751)). Applied to a bounded fusion task, this predicts precisely the observed symptom — the model stops once it has produced a sequence that is "good enough" under its length-normalized beam score, dropping whichever facts it hasn't yet verbalized rather than extending the output to include them. Layered on top of the length bias is an attention-allocation problem: Manakul and Gales directly measured encoder-decoder cross-attention in transformer summarizers and found it is **sparse and concentrated on a small subset of source sentences**, not spread evenly ([Manakul & Gales, EMNLP 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.739)) — a plausible mechanism by which facts outside the attention-dominant region are architecturally starved of the signal needed to surface at all, with smaller models (fewer heads, smaller hidden dimension) having less spare capacity to maintain separate "coverage state" per fact.

Finally, the training incentive itself never taught the model that exhaustive coverage matters, because the broader summarization literature it inherits behavior from is built on data where it doesn't. Wolhandler et al. measured that in MultiNews (4 source documents per topic), **a single document alone covers ~70% of summary content and two documents cover ~95%** — meaning even the human reference summaries models are trained to imitate rarely require genuine multi-source fusion ([Wolhandler et al., EMNLP 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.12688)). Lebanoff et al. found that only ~6% of a Pointer-Generator's output sentences were produced via genuine fusion of multiple source sentences, versus 32% in human-written abstracts — fusion is a skill the end-to-end training objective simply never rewards strongly enough to develop ([Lebanoff et al., EMNLP 2019 Workshop](https://research.adobe.com/publication/analyzing-sentence-fusion-in-abstractive-summarization/)). A bounded task with several short, roughly equally-weighted, non-redundant facts is exactly the structure this literature says models handle worst: there is no "dominant" fact to privilege, and the model's nearest learned analogy — single-document summarization, where partial coverage is normal and rewarded — pulls it toward picking a subset. None of these three mechanisms (length/brevity prior, attention sparsity, single-source-dominance training incentive) require any kind of "error" in the sense of a wrong token prediction; omission is close to the model's optimal behavior under its own training objective, which is precisely why it persists so robustly.

## Four fix families, each patching a different layer

The literature offers four distinct intervention points, and they do not compete so much as target different links in the causal chain above — which matters for deciding what to combine.

| Approach | What it changes | Strength | Key limitation for this task |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copy mechanisms (pointer-generator, PROM) | Model architecture: adds an explicit "lift this span" path | Directly targets verbatim preservation of names/numbers; See et al.'s pointer-generator beat prior abstractive SOTA by ≥2 ROUGE on CNN/DailyMail ([See, Liu & Manning, ACL 2017](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1704.04368)) | No source retrofits a copy mechanism onto T5/FLAN-T5 specifically — would require custom implementation and retraining, and risks trading hallucination for excessive extractiveness ("copy-too-much") |
| Constrained decoding (NeuroLogic, HF `force_words_ids`) | Decoding algorithm only, no retraining | Model-agnostic, works with any token-by-token generator including T5; Dynamic Beam Allocation achieves O(1) cost in constraint count ([Post & Vilar, NAACL 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1804.06609)) | Only guarantees *inclusion*, not correct placement or fluency — HF forum reports show "constraint satisfied but still wrong" outputs; HF's native support has drifted into an opt-in `trust_remote_code=True` community package, a stability caveat |
| Faithfulness-aware reranking / best-of-N | Decoding-time selection among sampled candidates, no retraining | Wan et al. demonstrated this directly at a comparable scale: on BART-large/PEGASUS-large (406–568M params), reranking beam candidates by a composite faithfulness score measurably improved FactCC and DAE scores over single-path decoding ([Wan et al., EACL 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.03278)) | The selection *signal*, not pool size, is the bottleneck — oracle-best faithfulness in the pool rose with beam width 2→10, but the *selected* top candidate improved only marginally. Nucleus/temperature sampling, often assumed to add helpful diversity, was *less* faithful than beam search in this study (FactCC 54 vs. 84), undercutting the common "sample more diversely" intuition |
| Narrow fine-tuning on fusion-shaped data | Training distribution itself | Closest analogue (DiscoFuse sentence fusion) constructs targets *guaranteed by design* to contain all source content, directly fixing the training-incentive gap described above ([DiscoFuse](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.10526)); cycle-training reaches near-fully-supervised faithfulness on WebNLG from just 100 labeled examples ([Cycle Training, arXiv 2305.14793](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.14793)) | No source fine-tunes flan-t5-small on exactly this task shape and reports a fact-recall delta — this specific experiment has not been published |

The best-of-N literature carries an important warning that cuts across all four approaches: whether sampling more candidates helps at all depends on whether the model's failure is **systematic (model-level) or stochastic (random variance)**. Work on beam search degeneracy found degenerate outputs are "mainly attributed to model errors instead of search errors" ([Investigating Label Bias in Beam Search, arXiv 2005.11009](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.11009)), and a 2026 analysis of test-time compute found that beam-search-style selection over a noisy scorer introduces an overestimation bias that *grows* with pool size past a "critical width" specific to the scorer's own noise level ([More Test-Time Compute Can Hurt, arXiv 2603.15377](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15377)). If flan-t5-small reliably drops the *same* fact across independent samples because its training-induced brevity prior always triggers at roughly the same point, generating more candidates will mostly reproduce the same omission — and no amount of reranking fixes a bias shared across the whole pool. This is testable directly and cheaply: sample N candidates at nonzero temperature and check whether the specific omitted fact varies across samples; if it doesn't, the defect is systematic and the fix belongs upstream (fine-tuning or constrained decoding), not in reranking. Separately, any best-of-N or constrained-decoding scheme is only as good as its verifier: the ROC-curve analysis of rejection sampling found both best-of-N and rejection sampling converge to the *same ceiling* at high compute, set by the verifier's own error rate, not by N ([ROC-n-reroll / Dorner et al., arXiv 2510.04087](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.04087v2)) — so validating the false-negative rate of whatever faithfulness gate is used matters as much as, or more than, choosing N.

## This is a genuine gap, not a known fix applied wrong

It is worth being explicit about where current research does and doesn't cover this exact problem shape, because the honest answer shapes what to try first. On the "solved elsewhere" side: faithfulness *detection* is mature and cheap to run — SummaC-style zero-shot NLI cross-encoders need no fine-tuning and run sub-second per sentence on CPU with base/ALBERT-scale models ([SummaC, TACL 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.tacl-1.10); [WikiCheck CPU latency](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.00835)), and generation-side reranking by a faithfulness score is validated at a directly comparable model scale (Wan et al.'s 406–568M BART/PEGASUS experiments). Data-to-text research — the closest subfield to "verbalize a fixed set of structured facts" — has decades of purpose-built metrics (PARENT, designed specifically to penalize hallucinated and reward faithfully-covered content) and working low-data recipes like cycle-training. On the "not solved" side: no leaderboard or benchmark tests sub-1B faithfulness as a primary axis in the way reasoning benchmarks do for math — the Vectara hallucination leaderboard's smallest entries are ~3-4B parameters, excluding T5/BART-scale models entirely ([Vectara leaderboard](https://github.com/vectara/hallucination-leaderboard/)). More pointedly, no source found tests the literal task framing — combine N discrete, non-redundant, already-verified facts into one fluent passage, preserving all of them, at sub-1B parameter scale — with a fact-recall metric. DiscoFuse-style sentence fusion is the nearest published proxy, but it operates on naturally-split sentence pairs recovered by rule, not an arbitrary curated list of independent facts, and even that literature reports fluency/SARI scores rather than per-fact recall. One genuinely counter-intuitive finding reinforces that this isn't simply a "use a bigger model" problem within the small-model band: a 2025 study found that generic instruction-tuning actively **reduced** factual consistency relative to a non-instruction-tuned base of the same size — Qwen2-0.5B-Instruct scored 89.0% average factual consistency versus 94.5% for its base, non-chat counterpart, falling further to 78.2% under more complex prompts ([Evaluating Small Language Models for News Summarization, arXiv 2502.00641](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.00641)). flan-t5-small sits in exactly this category — a generically instruction-tuned small checkpoint — so the very property that makes it convenient to prompt off-the-shelf is evidence against, not for, its faithfulness on a narrow task. Specialized, less "chatty" architectures (Pegasus-Large at 99.9%, Brio at 94.3% average factual consistency, both sub-600M and neither generically instruction-tuned) outperform general small instruct models by a wide margin in the same study, which argues that **task specialization, not raw scale, is the lever that actually moves faithfulness at this size** — directly undercutting the premise that this is "purely a scale problem."

## What to try next, in order, staying CPU-feasible

Given that the root cause is a training-distribution mismatch (the model was never taught that exhaustive, non-redundant multi-fact coverage is the goal) compounded by a decoding-time brevity bias, the highest-expected-value, lowest-compute path is to fix the training signal first and treat decoding-time tricks as a safety net, not the primary mechanism:

**First — build a small, DiscoFuse-style synthetic fine-tuning set and LoRA-adapt flan-t5-small on it.** Take existing fluent paragraphs, mechanically decompose them into their constituent factual sentences (the inverse of DiscoFuse's split-and-reconstruct recipe), and train the model to reconstruct the fluent version from the fact list, so the target is faithful-by-construction. This directly targets the single clearest root cause (a training incentive that never rewarded exhaustive coverage) and is mechanically cheap: LoRA fine-tuning of flan-t5-small trains well under 1% of parameters and is documented as already working on exactly this checkpoint ([flan-t5-small-summary-peft](https://huggingface.co/gnokit/flan-t5-small-summary-peft)), and cycle-training evidence suggests meaningful gains are achievable from as few as ~100 labeled/constructed examples. This is the one place where the evidence is analogical rather than direct — no paper runs this exact experiment — but it is also the one intervention that addresses the training-objective mismatch rather than papering over it at inference time, and it is the most promising place to close the literature's biggest identified gap.

**Second — add a cheap NLI-based post-hoc verifier as a gate regardless of what else is done.** A SummaC-ZS-style zero-shot entailment check (or a compact model like MiniTrue) adds sub-second CPU latency per candidate and requires no training; it should wrap *any* generation path as a final correctness check, since no fine-tuning result found drives faithfulness to 100% (even Pegasus-Large's best-in-class figure is 99.9%, not 100%). Pair it with a small best-of-N (N≈3–5, generated via beam search or diverse beam search, *not* plain nucleus sampling, since nucleus sampling was measured as the least faithful decoding strategy in the one comparable study) — but first run the cheap diagnostic described above (do independent samples disagree on which fact gets dropped?) to confirm the failure is stochastic enough for sampling to help before investing effort here.

**Third — layer in lexical constrained decoding (HF `force_words_ids` / Dynamic Beam Allocation) specifically for atomic, non-paraphrasable spans** such as numbers, dates, names, or citation markers that must survive verbatim — this is cheap (near-zero overhead with Dynamic Beam Allocation), requires no retraining, and is a good complement to fine-tuning rather than a substitute for it, since it only guarantees inclusion, not correct integration. Favor vendoring the specific implementation rather than depending on the `trust_remote_code=True` community package, given documented drift in HF's native support.

**Deprioritize retrofitting an explicit copy mechanism onto T5/FLAN-T5.** It is architecturally plausible and would likely help most with verbatim-span preservation, but no existing implementation exists for the T5 family, it requires nontrivial custom architecture work and retraining, and constrained decoding achieves a similar practical effect (forcing inclusion of specific spans) with far less engineering cost for this specific CPU-feasible, narrow-task setting. It's worth revisiting only if the fine-tuning-plus-constrained-decoding combination still under-preserves specific atomic facts after the above steps are tried.

## Conclusion

The deeper insight this research surfaces is that "small model hallucination" and "small model omission" are not the same failure with different names — the evidence here is specifically about a model declining to say things it was given, not inventing things it wasn't, and that distinction points toward a different toolkit than the hallucination-mitigation literature usually assumes. Most faithfulness research (constrained decoding, copy mechanisms, NLI verifiers) was built to stop models from adding or distorting content; it transfers to the omission problem only partially, because a model that confidently and fluently writes a *subset* of the truth doesn't trip most of those safeguards — there's nothing false to detect, and nothing needs to be "copied" versus hallucinated, since what's missing was never attempted. That asymmetry is why narrow, coverage-guaranteed fine-tuning data is the most promising lever here rather than decoding-time machinery borrowed from the hallucination literature: it is the only intervention in this report that directly supplies the model with a training signal saying "all of this is required," which is the signal every stage of T5's actual training history — pretraining, instruction-tuning, and beam search — has consistently withheld.
